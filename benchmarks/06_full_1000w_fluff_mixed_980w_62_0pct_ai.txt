Solid-state lithium-metal batteries deliver higher specific energy than conventional liquid-electrolyte cells. They also eliminate flammable organic solvents found in commercial lithium-ion packs. Pure metallic lithium anodes offer a theoretical capacity of 3,860 mAh g⁻¹. They also provide an ultra-low reduction potential. Transportation sectors and grid-scale storage facilities accelerate this transition. Fleet electrification continually increases the demand for stable, high-capacity chemistries. Yet practical adoption stalls at the interface. Severe chemo-mechanical degradation destroys solid-state cells during early cycling. Fast charging accelerates this breakdown. Unstable interphases grow between metallic lithium anodes and solid inorganic electrolytes during routine operation. These dynamic layers evolve unpredictably under voltage bias. Prolonged galvanostatic cycling triggers two concurrent failure modes. Lithium stripping creates microscopic voids along the contact boundary. Subsequent plating drives metallic dendrites across ceramic grain boundaries. Contact loss ruins performance. Localized current density spikes focus ionic flux into microscopic hotspots. Mechanical stress concentrates around interfacial defects and surface voids. Uneven interfacial contact worsens these hot spots. These combined forces trigger catastrophic electrical short circuits. Structural integrity collapses quickly. Interfacial decay caps practical cycle life and rate capability. Resolving these limitations requires a unified analysis of chemo-mechanical dynamics across atomistic and microscopic scales.

Coupled chemo-mechanical physics governs solid-electrolyte interfaces across disparate spatial and temporal scales. Our diagnostic measurements reveal clear degradation pathways governing cell operational lifetime. Baseline unengineered lithium-garnet symmetrical cells exhibited rapid interfacial impedance escalation during initial cycling. Area-specific resistance jumped 312% across the first fifty cycles at a current density of 1.0 mA cm⁻². Cell resistance skyrocketed quickly. Synchrotron tomographic reconstructions confirmed that void coalescence during stripping phases caused cell failure. Tomographic slices showed that void nucleation initiated at local contact edges. The active boundary receded continuously under sustained current drain. This coalescing process cut active electrochemical contact area by up to 43.8% across the interfacial plane. Voids severed contact paths. Depositing a five-nanometer aluminum oxide buffer layer suppressed void nucleation and stabilized charge transfer. Cells with this engineered interlayer maintained an area-specific resistance of 18.4 Ω·cm² after three hundred continuous cycles. This reduced interfacial resistance growth by 82.6% compared to unengineered baseline cells. The coating preserved contact. Coatings prevented dendrite shorts.

These empirical results establish foundational operating guidelines for commercial solid-state cell manufacturing. Nano-engineered interlayers and surface coatings provide essential electrochemical stability. Yet coatings cannot function without synchronized mechanical pressure control.
