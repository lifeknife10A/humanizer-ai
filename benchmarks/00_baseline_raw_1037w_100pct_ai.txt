Extended Abstract: Chemo-Mechanical Degradation Dynamics and Interfacial Stability in All-Solid-State Lithium-Metal Batteries: Multi-Scale Continuum Modeling and Operando Synchrotron Diagnostics

Solid-state lithium-metal batteries are playing an increasingly pivotal role in transforming the landscape of modern electrochemical energy storage systems, offering significantly enhanced energy densities and superior safety profiles compared to conventional liquid-electrolyte lithium-ion configurations. As transportation and grid-scale storage sectors accelerate electrification efforts, the demand for high-capacity battery chemistries has surged exponentially. However, the widespread commercial deployment of all-solid-state architectures remains fundamentally hindered by severe chemo-mechanical and interfacial degradation phenomena that occur during rapid charge-discharge cycling. A critical concern within contemporary materials science pertains to the unstable evolution of the solid-electrolyte interphase layer formed between metallic lithium anodes and solid inorganic electrolytes. When solid-state cells undergo prolonged galvanostatic cycling, microscopic void formation, contact loss, and lithium dendrite propagation inherently jeopardize electrochemical performance and structural integrity. Disparities in local current density, mechanical stress concentrations, and uneven interfacial contact frequently accelerate catastrophic short-circuit failures. Consequently, unconstrained interfacial degradation limits the practical cycle life and rate capability of solid-state cells. This investigation presents a comprehensive multi-scale analysis combining continuum electrochemical modeling, operando X-ray tomographic microscopy, and synchrotron spectroscopy to elucidate the degradation mechanisms governing solid-state lithium-metal interfaces.

The fundamental physics governing solid-electrolyte interfaces involves complex, tightly coupled chemo-mechanical interactions operating across disparate spatial and temporal scales. In standard electrochemical transport models, interfacial ion flux is parameterized using classical Butler-Volmer kinetics assuming planar, defect-free electrode boundaries. While this conventional theoretical formulation captures macro-scale thermodynamic behaviors, it completely overlooks microscopic surface roughness, localized stress accumulation, and non-uniform electrodeposition kinetics. Furthermore, solid electrolyte matrices are notoriously vulnerable to mechanical fracture, intergranular crack propagation, and anisotropic grain boundary conductivity, all of which compound interfacial degradation under high current densities. To address these theoretical deficiencies, this study constructs a coupled elastoplastic phase-field framework that integrates vacancy diffusion, interfacial stress evolution, and electrochemical reaction kinetics. Specifically, we investigate the thermodynamic driving forces that govern void nucleation during lithium stripping and dendrite penetration during plating cycles. By formalizing a generalized chemical potential function that explicitly incorporates elastic energy strain, interfacial surface tension, and electrostatic overpotential, we delineate the critical operating boundaries required to suppress morphological instability and prevent mechanical failure within solid-state architectures.

To empirically validate the continuum theoretical model, a multi-modal operando characterization campaign was executed across a diverse series of solid-state symmetrical and full-cell configurations. Symmetrical lithium-garnet cells were fabricated utilizing dense ceramic lithium lanthanum zirconium tantalum oxide pellets synthesized via high-temperature spark plasma sintering. High-resolution operando synchrotron X-ray computed tomography was conducted at the beamline facility to visualize real-time three-dimensional morphological changes occurring at the buried lithium-electrolyte interface during continuous cycling. Concurrently, operando Raman spectroscopy, time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy were deployed to monitor chemical species evolution, interfacial resistance growth, and interphasial composition dynamics. Furthermore, three distinct interfacial engineering strategies were systematically implemented to evaluate their comparative protective efficacy, including atomic layer deposition of ultra-thin aluminum oxide interlayers, in-situ chemical formation of lithiophilic metal alloy interphases, and mechanical pressure modulation ranging from 0.5 to 7.0 megapascals. In-situ pressure monitoring was maintained continuously utilizing high-precision load cells integrated directly into the custom-designed experimental test fixtures.

The empirical observations generated from this multi-scale diagnostic campaign provide vital mechanistic insights regarding the operational lifetime and degradation pathways of solid-state batteries. Baseline unengineered lithium-garnet symmetrical cells exhibited rapid interfacial impedance escalation, with area-specific resistance increasing by 312% over the initial fifty cycles at a current density of 1.0 milliampere per square centimeter. Synchrotron tomographic reconstructions revealed that critical failure was precipitated by extensive void coalescence during stripping phases, which reduced active electrochemical contact area by up to 43.8% across the interfacial plane. However, the introduction of a five-nanometer atomic-layer-deposited aluminum oxide buffer layer dramatically suppressed void nucleation and stabilized interfacial charge transfer. Cells incorporating this engineered interphase demonstrated an area-specific resistance of only 18.4 ohm-square centimeters after three hundred continuous cycles, representing an 82.6% reduction in interfacial resistance growth relative to unengineered baselines. Notably, operando chemical mapping confirmed that the conformal metal alloy interphase successfully harmonized local current distributions, thereby raising the critical current density threshold for dendrite initiation from 1.2 to 3.8 milliamperes per square centimeter.

The broader technological implications of these experimental and computational findings extend substantially beyond laboratory-scale diagnostic cells, offering foundational guidelines for the industrial design and manufacturing of solid-state batteries. While nano-engineered interlayers and surface coatings provide essential electrochemical stabilization, they cannot serve as standalone solutions without synchronized external mechanical pressure management. Unoptimized external stack pressures can inadvertently accelerate interfacial creep or induce mechanical cracking throughout the brittle solid electrolyte separator. Furthermore, battery manufacturing consortia must establish rigorous standardized operando testing protocols that continuously monitor interfacial impedance drift and localized thermal fluctuations under dynamic driving cycles. Ensuring long-term durability through holistic multi-scale engineering, including grain boundary passivation, stoichiometric dopant optimization, and compliant interlayer design, is paramount for establishing commercial viability among electric vehicle original equipment manufacturers. Additionally, collaborative coordination between solid-state chemists, mechanical modeling engineers, material characterization specialists, and industrial cell producers is vital to establish scalable manufacturing processes capable of producing defect-free, high-area solid-state laminates.

Ultimately, this study highlights the transformative potential of solid-state lithium-metal batteries while elucidating the critical chemo-mechanical constraints governing interfacial degradation. As clean energy storage requirements expand globally, the electrochemical research community must navigate the complex trade-offs between high energy density, fast-charging rate capability, and long-term interfacial stability with scientific precision. By linking continuum mathematical formulations directly to synchrotron operando diagnostics, this investigation provides a predictive theoretical framework and practical design roadmap for stabilizing solid-electrolyte interfaces under aggressive cycling regimes. Future research will delve into the development of self-healing composite polymer-ceramic electrolytes to accommodate volume fluctuations during extended deep-discharge cycles. In conclusion, the successful commercialization of all-solid-state batteries depends fundamentally on achieving a balanced synergy between atomistic interfacial engineering, continuum mechanical optimization, and scalable production methodologies.
