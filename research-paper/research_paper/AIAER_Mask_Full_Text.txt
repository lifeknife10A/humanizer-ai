Short Paper
AIAER-Mask: Safe Neuro-Symbolic Intent-Correct Routing for
Event-Driven Microservices
Hossam Hassan , Shadia Sarhan , and Ahmed Shalaby
Abstract—Event-driven microservice systems route heterogeneous
operations through rapidly changing service replicas. Conventional
performance-oriented routing systems, including static routing, EWMA-
based selection, Power-of-Two-Choices, and unconstrained reinforcement-
learning policies, can select low-latency replicas that are semantically in-
valid for the requested operation. Such routing mistakes may preserve raw
throughput while violating application intent, consistency, privacy bound-
aries, or update semantics. This paper presents AIAER-Mask, a safe neuro-
symbolic routing framework for intent-correct event routing. AIAER-Mask
separates safety from adaptation: a lightweight symbolic intent guard de-
rives a hard action mask from a capability map, while an ofﬂine discrete Soft
Actor-Critic policy selects among only admissible replicas using telemetry
features. The symbolic mask is not a neural model; it is a deterministic safety
layer that prevents invalid actions by construction under the active validated
map. The evaluation covers multi-replica routing, ablations, masked ver-
sus unmasked baselines, failure injection, dynamic/adversarial scenarios,
broker-delay and local region-latency emulation, hyperparameter sensi-
tivity, training diagnostics, reproducibility reporting, and DeathStarBench
SocialNetwork/HotelReservation cross-workload evaluation. Across both
workloads, hard symbolic masking preserves 100% intent correctness and
0% invalid actions for masked variants under validated and actively en-
forced capability maps, while performance-only no-mask baselines violate
semantic routing constraints heavily. The mask itself is low-overhead for
heuristic routers, whereas most additional overhead in full AIAER-Mask
comes from neural inference, telemetry/state processing, and feature han-
dling. Strong masked heuristics such as Mask+EWMA and Intent-safe
P2C remain highly competitive and sometimes outperform the learned
policy in latency and regret. We therefore position AIAER-Mask as a
safety-constrained neuro-symbolic framework, not as the fastest router
in every regime. The current evidence supports instantiation across two
single-region DeathStarBench workloads and local region-like latency pro-
ﬁles; broader production-trace, real multi-broker, and real multi-region
validation remains future work.
Index Terms —Event-driven architecture, microservices, safe routing,
neuro-symbolic systems, ofﬂine reinforcement learning, symbolic masking,
intent correctness, DeathStarBench, reproducibility.

This distinction exposes a weakness in common routing practice.
Static routing, EWMA latency routing, Power-of-Two-Choices, and
learned policies can all optimize latency while ignoring semantic ad-
missibility. A globally fast replica is not necessarily a valid replica
for the current operation. Treating intent correctness as a soft reward
penalty is also insufﬁcient, because a low-latency invalid action can
still be selected when the learned policy is uncertain, the reward is
misspeciﬁed, or the deployment workload shifts. In safety-sensitive or
consistency-sensitive microservice systems, the correct question is not
only which service is fastest, but which services are allowed.
Existing work on cloud routing, microservice load balancing, and
reinforcement learning for systems has shown that adaptive policies
can improve resource use under changing load [5], [8], [9]. Ofﬂine
reinforcement learning avoids unsafe online exploration by learning
from historical behavior logs[3], [4]. Intent-based networking and zero-

mechanism that guarantees semantic admissibility for every routing
action while still allowing adaptive performance optimization.
We propose AIAER-Mask, a neuro-symbolic routing framework that
separates what must never happen from what should be optimized.
The symbolic layer maintains a capability map C(a,o ) that declares
whether action or replica a may serve operation o. This layer produces
a hard mask over invalid actions. The neural layer, implemented as
an ofﬂine discrete Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) policy [25], ranks candidate
actions using latency, queue, load, success-rate, and stress features. The
learned policy is permitted to adapt only within the symbolic safety
envelope.
The evidence supports a nuanced interpretation: AIAER-Mask pro-
vides strict intent correctness through hard symbolic masking under a
validated and actively enforced capability map, while strong masked
heuristics such as Mask+EWMA and Intent-safe P2C can remain
competitive or outperform the learned policy in some latency and regret
regimes. The strongest contribution is that hard symbolic masking
provides strict intent correctness at very low masking cost, while
the learned policy offers a path toward adaptive safety-constrained
routing as environments become larger, more dynamic, and more
non-stationary.

II. R ELA TEDWORK
A. Microservice and Event-Driven Routing
Microservice routing is commonly implemented through static ta-
bles, round-robin, least-connections, EWMA latency routing, and ran-
domized load balancing such as Power-of-Two-Choices [2]. Heuristic
and metaheuristic load-balancing methods remain attractive because
they are inexpensive and transparent [7]. In event-driven systems,
message brokers such as NA TS, Kafka, or RabbitMQ add decoupling
between producers and consumers, but they do not automatically solve
semantic routing correctness. A broker may deliver an event efﬁciently
while the application-level router still chooses a semantically invalid
handler.
B. Reinforcement Learning for Cloud Systems
Reinforcement learning has been studied for cluster scheduling,
resource allocation, load balancing, and microservice placement [5],
[6], [8], [9]. These systems can adapt to changing load, but online
exploration can be unsafe in production. More importantly for this work,
many formulations optimize performance metrics over anonymous
requests and do not encode semantic operation constraints. An RL
router without a hard safety layer may learn that an invalid but fast
action is attractive.
C. Ofﬂine RL and Safe Deployment
Ofﬂine RL learns from logged data without online exploration [3],
[4]. Conservative methods such as CQL, IQL, and TD3+BC reduce out-
of-distribution action selection [10], [11], [12]. Off-policy evaluation
and Fitted Q Evaluation help screen policies before deployment [13],
[14]. These techniques improve deployment discipline, but they do not
by themselves guarantee semantic admissibility. AIAER-Mask uses
ofﬂine RL for adaptation, while a separate symbolic guard enforces
admissibility.
D. Safe RL, Constrained Decisions, and Action Masking
Safe RL and constrained decision systems distinguish reward opti-
mization from constraint satisfaction. Invalid action masking has been
shown to improve learning in structured action spaces by removing
illegal choices from the policy distribution [18], [19], [20], [21].O u r
setting differs from games or schedulers where invalidity is often syn-
tactic. Here, invalidity is semantic and application-speciﬁc: an action
is valid only if the service replica is capable of handling the operation
intent.
E. Intent-Based and Neuro-Symbolic Systems
Intent-based networking maps operator goals to network behavior
[15], [16], [17]. Neuro-symbolic systems combine learned models with
symbolic constraints or reasoning layers [22], [23], [24]. AIAER-Mask
follows this hybrid design principle, but applies it to event-driven
microservice replica selection. The symbolic component is deliberately
simple and auditable; the neural component is used for performance
adaptation inside the safe region.
F . MLOps and Reproducible AI Systems
Deploying learned policies in systems infrastructure introduces op-
erational concerns beyond model accuracy. MLOps work emphasizes
versioned data pipelines, monitored deployment, reproducible environ-
ments, and model lifecycle management [26], [27], [28]. These con-
cerns are directly relevant to learned routing: stale telemetry, topology
drift, capability-map changes, and policy-update cadence can all affect
safety and performance. The experiment pipeline therefore records
environment details, seeds, repeated trials, dataset provenance, warm-
up ﬁltering, hyperparameter sensitivity, and operational-complexity
measurements.

Proposition 1(Intent correctness under hard symbolic masking):
Assume that (i) the capability map C(a,o ) is correct for the active
deployment conﬁguration, (ii) the router applies the hard mask M (o)
before every action selection, (iii) the action selector samples or chooses
only from the masked action set, and (iv) unknown operations or empty
valid-action sets are rejected or sent to fallback rather than guessed.
Then every selected non-fallback action at satisﬁesC(at,ot)=1 .
Proof sketch: For every invalid action a with C(a,ot)=0 ,t h e
hard mask removes the action from the admissible set or assigns it a
blocking logit. The stochastic policy therefore assigns zero probability
to invalid actions, and a deterministic selector cannot choose them
because it operates only over V (ot). Thus, an invalid non-fallback
action can be selected only if the capability map is wrong, the mask
is bypassed, or an implementation fault violates the assumptions. The
argument is a formal safety argument for the implemented masking
rule, not a machine-checked proof of the whole deployment. Full formal
veriﬁcation remains future work.
IV . AIAER-MASK ARCHITECTURE
A. Separation of Symbolic and Neural Layers
AIAER-Mask has two distinct layers:
r Symbolic safety layer: This layer stores the capability map, val-
idates operation-action admissibility, constructs the hard mask,
and blocks invalid cross-operation routing. It is deterministic,
rule-based, auditable, and lightweight.
r Neural adaptive layer: This layer is an ofﬂine-trained discrete
SAC policy. It receives telemetry-derived state features and selects
among the valid replicas that remain after masking.
The symbolic mask is not an AI model. It is a lightweight symbolic
safety layer that constrains the action space before the learned policy
acts.
Fig. 1 summarizes the proposed neuro-symbolic routing pipeline.
The symbolic layer ﬁrst ﬁlters the action space through the capability
map, after which the learned policy ranks only admissible replicas using
telemetry-derived state features.

C. Training Stabilization
The ofﬂine learner uses discrete SAC with behavior-cloning regular-
ization and regret-aware reward shaping. Behavior cloning is applied
only to logged behavior actions that are valid under the active capability
map. Regret-aware shaping uses telemetry snapshots in the baseline
behavior logs to estimate the latency gap between the selected action and
the best valid replica. No ﬁnal-evaluation outputs are used for training.
V. D A TASET,I MPLEMENTA TION, AND REPRODUCIBILITY
A. Implementation Platform
The implementation uses DeathStarBench SocialNetwork and
HotelReservation [1] with Dockerized services and a NA TS-based
event fabric [29]. SocialNetwork is the primary workload for the full
experiment pipeline. Its logical action space contains 18 actions across
6 operation groups, with 3 valid replicas per operation. The Social-
Network capability map is externalized in a Y AML conﬁguration ﬁle.
V alidation reports 6 operations, 18 actions, 18 valid operation-action
pairs, 90 invalid pairs, no unmapped operations, and no zero-valid-
action operation. HotelReservation uses a separate workload-speciﬁc
Y AML capability map to evaluate whether the symbolic mask can be
instantiated for a second DeathStarBench service topology. The valida-
tor emits a map hash and machine-readable report, and the evaluated
maps include a schema_version ﬁeld so that capability maps can
be treated as versioned deployment artifacts.
B. Ofﬂine Dataset Collection
The ofﬂine dataset is built only from dedicated behavior-policy base-
line CSVs, not from ﬁnal evaluation logs. The behavior policies include
Intent-EWMA, H-LRU, H-A∗, Op-Static, P2C, LEW, and Intent-safe
P2C under low, medium, and high stress levels, with ﬁve seeds.
Additional training perturbations include queue-depth spike, regional
latency spike, and burst load under high stress. The data collection
report records 210 baseline CSV ﬁles and 27,227,991 loaded rows. To
keep the monolithic training artifact manageable, the builder writes a
deterministic 1,000,000-transition training sample from the available
baseline transitions and records the sampling metadata. Logged ﬁelds
include operation, selected action, latency, success, overhead, timeout,
application error, active and true capability validity, invalid selections,
fallback indicators, warm-up ﬂags, and telemetry snapshots.

A. Research Questions
The evaluation addresses the following questions:
r RQ1: Does hard symbolic masking improve intent correctness
and invalid-action prevention?
r RQ2: Does the mask itself introduce meaningful operational
overhead?
r RQ3: How does AIAER-Mask compare with strong masked

r RQ4: How robust is the hard-mask design under failure injection
and stale or abnormal conditions?
r RQ5: What are the reproducibility, hyperparameter, stress-
calibration, broker-dependency, and region-latency boundaries?
r RQ6: Can the same symbolic masking mechanism be instantiated
across more than one DeathStarBench workload?

C. Cross-Workload Evaluation
We instantiate AIAER-Mask on two DeathStarBench workloads:
SocialNetwork and HotelReservation. Each workload uses its own
capability map that deﬁnes valid operation-action pairs for that appli-
cation. The same evaluation principles are applied across workloads:
capability-map validation, hard symbolic masking, masked versus no-
mask comparison, stable evaluation, safety metrics, and repeated seeded
trials where applicable. The goal is not to claim universal applicability,
but to test whether the capability-map-based symbolic mask preserves
intent correctness across different DeathStarBench microservice struc-

D. Stress, Broker , and Region-Latency Sensitivity
The offered-load tiers are conﬁgured as 5k, 9k, and 14k requests/s
for low, medium, and high stress. The calibration report validates that
these are ordered offered-load tiers and observes latency and p99 spread
across tiers. We do not claim these values were derived from a full
empirical saturation search. Broker dependency is evaluated through
broker-delay emulation at 0, 2, 5, 10, and 20 ms. This is a broker-delay
sensitivity study, not a real multi-broker comparison across NA TS,
Kafka, and RabbitMQ. We also add Local Region-Latency Emula-
tion: services remain local, while deterministic router/environment-
layer latency proﬁles approximate same-region, near-region, far-region,
asymmetric, degraded, recovering, stale-telemetry, and bursty region-
like conditions. These proﬁles test timing sensitivity and adapta-
tion under local emulation only; they are not a real multi-region
deployment.
E. Dynamic and Safety Scenarios
Dynamic adaptation scenarios include sudden degradation, recov-
ery, queue spike, rotating hotspot, bursty workload, mixed operation
pressure, partial failure, oscillating latency, misleading EWMA, simul-
taneous degradation, and delayed recovery. A separate environment
validation report conﬁrms non-stationarity before routing results are
interpreted: every scenario is labeled valid_dynamic, the oracle
best replica changes over time, and best-versus-worst valid-replica
latency gaps are measurable.
Safety scenarios include unknown operation label, invalid service
capability, service instance failure, stale capability map, burst load,
queue-depth spike, and regional/network latency spike. These tests
distinguish active capability validity from true capability validity, which
is important when capability metadata is stale.
F . Metrics
We report mean latency (ms), p99 latency (ms), throughput
(events/s), routing overhead (μs/request), success rate (%), intent cor-
rectness (%), invalid action rate (%), invalid action count, fallback
count, cumulative regret (ms), optimal replica match (%), adaptation
time (ms), and recovery time (ms). Success rate is not the same as
intent correctness: a request can complete at the system level while
being semantically misrouted.

The multi-replica capability map validates successfully: 6 oper-

operation-action pairs. No unmapped operation, zero-valid-action op-
eration, or unassigned service capability is present in the validated map.
Under normal evaluation, all hard-masked variants report 100% active
capability validity and 0% invalid-action rate.

D. Safety-Performance Tradeoff Interpretation
The central empirical lesson is that correctness and performance
must be interpreted jointly. The masked versus no-mask analysis indi-
cates that simple masked baselines incur an average overhead change of
1.548μs/request, while the AIAER/SAC hard-mask comparisons show
an average overhead change of 335.190 μs/request. This separates the
cost of the symbolic intent-guard layer from the cost of the neural
adaptive layer.
The correctness guarantee comes primarily from symbolic masking:
invalid actions are removed before either a heuristic or learned policy se-
lects a route. The mask is rule-based, deterministic, and capability-map
constrained. By contrast, the learned SAC layer contributes adaptive
ranking within the valid action set, but also introduces inference,
feature-construction, telemetry, and state-processing overhead. This
explains why masked heuristics remain extremely competitive in the
current results. It also clariﬁes the expected deployment envelope: as
the environment becomes larger, more delayed, more non-stationary,
or more multi-region, the value of adaptive learning may increase, but
the current single-region setup remains structured enough for strong
telemetry heuristics to perform well.
Fig. 4 summarizes the safety-performance tradeoff. The safe variants
cluster at 100% intent correctness, whereas performance-only baselines
can appear competitive in latency while violating semantic constraints.

G. Ablation, Hyperparameters, and Training Convergence
The ablation study isolates the role of hard masking, soft penalties,
static masked routing, EWMA masked routing, and reduced state
features. The key result is that soft penalties and no-mask SAC do
not guarantee safety. Training is evaluated using held-out diagnostics
rather than a reward-only curve.
Fig. 8 presents validation regret rather than raw reward. This supports
the training-stability discussion, while safety remains enforced sepa-
rately by Proposition 1 and the hard symbolic mask under the validated
capability map.
The training diagnostics are optimization diagnostics rather than
safety evidence. Because the reward combines latency, regret, time-
out, and regularization terms, average reward can remain negative or
visually stable even when validation regret and optimal-match behavior
improve. We therefore assess training stability using validation regret,
policy entropy, optimal-valid-replica match, critic/Q-loss stability, and
held-out routing metrics. Safety is enforced separately by Proposition1
and the hard symbolic mask under a validated capability map. Final test
metrics are not used for hyperparameter tuning.
The hyperparameter study varies learning rate, batch size, hid-
den units, discount factor, entropy temperature, behavior-cloning co-
efﬁcient, and regret coefﬁcient. Each conﬁguration records training
outcomes and routing metrics. The ﬁnal pipeline uses a determinis-

sensitivity analysis, while ﬁnal test measurements are not used for
tuning. The training audit records source-log, conﬁguration, dataset,
and capability-map hashes; duplicate-curve checks; entropy; valida-
tion regret; optimal-valid-replica match; and invalid-action diagnostics
where available. Thus, convergence is interpreted through validation
diagnostics and routing metrics rather than reward alone.

I. Key Takeaways
Three takeaways follow from the evidence. First, hard symbolic
masking converts intent correctness from a reward preference into a
routing invariant under the validated capability map. Second, unsafe
no-mask routing can appear fast because it may choose semantically
invalid replicas. Third, the learned AIAER-Mask layer is most valuable
under harder timing conditions: stable settings keep Mask+EWMA,
Mask+Static, and Intent-safe P2C strong, while Local Region-Latency
Emulation shows clearer adaptive value in regret and optimal-valid-
replica match.
The artifact also includes a safety-assurance summary that links each
mechanism to its evidence and remaining assumption, including hard
masking, fallback handling, map validation, failure injection, and delay
emulation.

B. Why Use AIAER-Mask?
The strongest reason to use AIAER-Mask is not broad speed dom-
inance. The reason is safety-constrained learning. Pure performance-
only routing can be fast but unsafe. Soft penalties reduce but do not
eliminate invalid actions. Hard symbolic masking eliminates invalid
actions for known operations under the active capability map. The
learned layer then offers an adaptive routing mechanism within the
safe set.
The present results also show that masked heuristics are serious
baselines. In stable environments and in the current dynamic scenarios,
Mask+EWMA and Intent-safe P2C can match or outperform AIAER-
Mask on latency or regret while preserving correctness. This does not
weaken the symbolic masking contribution; rather, it clariﬁes that the
mask is broadly useful across both heuristic and learned routers.
C. Practical Implications
For practitioners, the safest near-term deployment pattern is layered.
A symbolic capability guard should be placed in front of any perfor-
mance optimizer, including simple heuristics, because it prevents entire
classes of semantic routing failures. If a system is relatively stable
and telemetry is fresh, masked heuristics may provide an attractive
low-overhead operating point. The full learned AIAER-Mask policy is
most appropriate when the routing environment is sufﬁciently dynamic
to justify the additional inference and feature-processing cost, and
when the operator can support model monitoring, ofﬂine retraining,
and capability-map governance.
D. Applicability
AIAER-Mask applies best to systems where operations have clear
semantic routing constraints and where invalid routing has real cor-
rectness, privacy, consistency, or compliance cost. The cross-workload
extension strengthens evidence that AIAER-Mask is not speciﬁc to one
DeathStarBench topology, because SocialNetwork and HotelReserva-
tion use different operation sets and workload-speciﬁc capability maps.
However, broader production traces and multi-region deployments re-
main future work. The framework is less compelling for homogeneous
stateless services where every replica is truly interchangeable, and
it requires a maintained capability map, telemetry collection, and an
ofﬂine training pipeline for the learned component.
E. Capability-Map Evolution
The current implementation treats the validated capability map as the
source of routing safety during an experiment. If an operation or action
is not present in that map, the router does not infer a new capability
at runtime. It rejects the event or sends it to the conﬁgured fallback
path and records the context for analysis. This conservative behavior
is deliberate: allowing a learned policy to promote an unknown action
directly into the active safety map would weaken the intent-correctness
guarantee.
Capability-map validation checks that every operation has at least
one valid action, every valid action exists in the action space, every
action belongs to a declared service capability group, no duplicate
or unknown actions appear, no service action is left unassigned, and
valid/invalid pair counts are nonempty. The generated validation artifact
records hashes for the SocialNetwork and HotelReservation maps,
supporting CI-style version tracking. In deployment, CI should re-
ject zero-valid actions, unknown actions, duplicate mappings, missing
schema ﬁelds, or owner mismatches. Capability-map changes should
require service-owner approval, CI validation, shadow/canary testing,
runtime monitoring, and rollback. AI-assisted discovery may cluster
repeated unknown-operation events and propose candidate updates,
but candidates must remain inactive until schema checks, policy rules,
service-owner review, shadow evaluation, and rollback planning suc-
ceed. Thus, learning may help maintain the map while the active hard
mask remains deterministic and auditable.
F . Limitations and Future Work
The present study provides bounded evidence rather than universal
deployment claims. It evaluates two DeathStarBench workloads, So-
cialNetwork and HotelReservation, which improves workload diversity
but remains benchmark-based rather than production-trace validation.
All experiments are single-region; Local Region-Latency Emulation
injects controlled region-like timing asymmetry and therefore strength-
ens timing stress coverage, but it does not replace real multi-region
deployment studies. Broker sensitivity is likewise delay emulation, not
a direct NA TS versus Kafka versus RabbitMQ deployment comparison.
The dynamic and failure-injection scenarios are validated and non-
stationary, but they remain controlled experiments rather than pro-
duction topology drift at scale. The current environment is also rela-
tively structured, and strong masked telemetry heuristics remain highly
competitive. Thus, the learned router should be viewed as a safety-
constrained adaptive framework whose beneﬁts are expected to become
more pronounced in harder delayed-telemetry, multi-region, larger-
scale, or stronger-drift settings. Future work should therefore exam-
ine additional workloads, production-derived traces, real multi-region
deployments, real broker ecosystems, larger action spaces, optimized
inference, and more efﬁcient telemetry pipelines.
Applicability depends on explicit semantic routing constraints and
maintainable capability maps. Although the map is validated and ver-
sioned, correctness of the real-world speciﬁcation remains a governance
responsibility. AI-assisted map evolution is promising, but candidate
updates should remain outside the active routing path until validated
through schema checks, policy checks, service-owner review, and safe
rollout. Taken together, we claim intent correctness under the active
validated capability map and tested scenarios, but not universal latency
superiority, real multi-broker generalization, or real multi-region pro-
duction readiness.
